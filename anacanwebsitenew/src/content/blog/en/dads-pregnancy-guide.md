Pregnancy is a journey for two — yet most dads describe the same feeling: "I don't know what to do." The good news: you don't need to be perfect to be supportive, you need concrete steps. This guide offers a practical "dad plan" for every trimester — from small daily gestures to running the logistics of birth day.

<li>In the 1st trimester, the biggest support is taking over chores and being patient</li>

## First trimester: the invisible-effort phase

The first 13 weeks look quiet from the outside, but for your partner it's one of the hardest stretches: nausea, deep fatigue and hormonal waves. There's no bump yet — but the need for support is at its peak.

**What you can do:** keep light snacks ready for morning sickness; take over the kitchen if food smells trigger nausea; never say "it's just tiredness" — this fatigue is real physiology; and be there for the first ultrasound, no excuses.

## Second trimester: the planning-together phase

Energy returns, the bump appears, and the pregnancy starts to feel "real". This is the golden window for shared decisions.

**What you can do:** join the anatomy scan (week 20) — you'll see your baby in detail for the first time; build name shortlists and vote ([Partner mode](/en/partner-mode/) has a tool for exactly this); plan the nursery together; start talking to the baby — hearing develops from week 24 and your voice will be recognized.

## Third trimester: project-manager mode

In the final weeks your partner's physical load grows: poor sleep, back pain, constant bathroom trips. Your role is to own the entire "technical" side of birth day.

**Birth-day preparation plan:**

1. Test the route to the hospital in advance — plus a backup route
2. Pack your own bag too (change of clothes, snacks, charger)
3. Learn the contraction timer — the 5-1-1 rule is your responsibility
4. Install the car seat ahead of time
5. Save the key numbers (doctor, hospital, taxi) in your phone

## Do dads change too?

Research suggests roughly 10% of fathers experience depressive symptoms during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Poor sleep, tension and the "I'm not ready" feeling are normal — talking about them is not weakness. Track your own mood too, and get support if you need it.
